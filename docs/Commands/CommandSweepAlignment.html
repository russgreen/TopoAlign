Sweep Along Alignment

Makes a road or path by sweeping a cross-section along an alignment. The road follows the alignment in plan and the alignment's profile in height, so it climbs, dips and rounds crests and sags with it.

Usage / Steps

  1. Draw the cross-section in a profile family (a Profile family template): one closed shape of lines and arcs, with the family's origin at the centreline of the road at finished level. Load the family into the project.
  2. Select an alignment first, or launch Sweep Along Alignment and click Pick alignment.
  3. Choose the Road section. The dialog reads the shape and shows its width and height.
  4. Set the section spacing, an optional vertical offset and the category, then click Create Road.

To sweep several profiles at once, for example a carriageway, a kerb on each side and a footway beyond each kerb, set Sweep to Assembly of profiles. See Assemblies below.

Options / Parameters

  • Road section — A profile family type loaded in the project. Its shape must be a single closed loop of straight lines and arcs. X across the family is across the road (positive to the right of the direction of travel) and Y is up.
  • Flip section left to right — Mirrors the shape, for a profile drawn the other way round.
  • Units — The units used for the spacing and offset.
  • Section spacing — Distance between sections along the alignment. Where the alignment curves or changes grade, extra sections are added automatically, and a section is always placed where a straight meets a curve.
  • Vertical offset — Raises (positive) or lowers (negative) the whole road relative to the alignment's profile.
  • Geometry — Segmented loft makes a solid between each pair of sections, which is robust but leaves a visible edge at every section that can't be hidden. Smooth surfaces makes continuous faces along the road (one spline per corner of the section, with a surface between neighbouring splines), so there are no edges across the road except where it is cut into a few long pieces at the ends of the alignment's horizontal curves. If Revit can't make the smooth surfaces, for example when the section is wider than the tightest curve, the road is made as a segmented loft and the summary says so.
  • Create as — Direct shape (the default) adds plain geometry. Family makes a Generic Model family from the road and places one instance of it, so the road has a family and a type in the Project Browser. Both are tagged with the alignment. Toposolid only (no road) (Revit 2024 and later) adds no road at all: the swept geometry is used only to make a toposolid that follows its top surface, using the Toposolid type and Level you choose. Nothing is tagged, so running it again makes another toposolid rather than replacing one, and any existing road for the alignment is left alone. To convert a road that already exists, use Sweep to Toposolid.
  • Category — The Revit category the road is filed under: Roads, Hardscape or Generic Models, whichever your version of Revit accepts. The last choice is remembered. A family uses the same category where the family editor allows it, otherwise Generic Models.

Assemblies

An assembly is a named list of parts. Each part is a profile family type placed at a lateral and a vertical offset from the alignment, and each part is swept along the alignment and made as its own element. Every part uses the same sections as the others, so they line up along the whole length.

  1. Set Sweep to Assembly of profiles and click Edit assemblies....
  2. Name the assembly, then add a row for each part (Add part) and fill in its columns. The picture on the right shows the outline of every part as it will stand across the road, so you can check the sides and heights before creating anything.
  3. Click Save, then Close, choose the assembly in the dialog and click Create Road Assembly.
  • Part — A name for the part, for example Kerb (left). It is kept in the comments of the element the part makes.
  • Profile — The profile family type swept for the part. It is drawn in the same way as a single road section, with the family's origin at the point that is placed on the offset.
  • Lateral offset — How far across the road the profile's origin is placed. Positive is to the right of the direction of travel and negative is to the left.
  • Vertical offset — How far up (positive) or down (negative) the origin is placed from the alignment's finished level.
  • Mirror — Flips the profile left to right about its own origin before it is placed. A kerb drawn for the left of the road needs this ticked when it is used on the right. Every part is defined explicitly, so a road with a kerb on each side has two kerb rows, one of them mirrored.
  • Category — The category the part is filed under, so the road, kerbs and footways can be scheduled, given materials and hidden separately.

Section spacing, geometry and the vertical offset in the sweep dialog apply to the whole assembly, and the vertical offset is added to each part's own. An assembly is always made as direct shapes. Running the command again for the same alignment replaces everything TopoAlign made for it before, whether that was a single road or an assembly.

Assemblies are saved as one JSON file each in %AppData%\TopoAlign\Assemblies, with lengths in millimetres, so you can copy a file to share an assembly. Parts refer to profile types by their name (Family : Type), so those types need to be loaded in the project; a part whose profile is missing is flagged and the assembly can't be built until it is fixed.

Grading the topo to meet the road

With an assembly, TopoAlign can also reshape an existing toposolid so the ground slopes from the edge of the road up or down to meet it (Revit 2024 and later). It uses the same method as Grade to Surface, once for each side of the alignment, and changes the toposolid only; no extra geometry is added.

  1. In the assembly editor, under Grade the topo out from the sides, tick Grade for the Left and/or Right side and set the slopes. Save the assembly.
  2. In the sweep dialog, with the assembly chosen, tick Grade the topo out from the sides, click Pick toposolids and pick every toposolid to reshape, then choose Finish. There can be a different toposolid either side of the road: each side changes the toposolids its slope reaches and leaves the others alone.
  3. Click Create Road Assembly. The road is made first, then the toposolid is graded, and the summary reports each side.
  • Cut slope — Used where the ground is above the edge of the road, so the slope climbs to meet it.
  • Fill slope — Used where the ground is below the edge of the road, so the slope falls away to meet it. Cut and fill are decided separately at every section, so one road can cut in one place and fill in another.
  • Slope as — How both slopes are entered: a ratio (1 : N, so 1 : 3 is entered as 3), a percentage or an angle in degrees.
  • Max width — The furthest a slope may run, measured square to the alignment, before giving up on that section.

The slope starts at the outer edge of the whole assembly on that side: the outermost point across the road of any part and, at that distance, its highest point. For a road with a kerb and footway this is the outer top edge of the footway. Each side is graded on its own, so turn off a side that doesn't need it. The toposolid's own points under the road are not changed, and the assembly's vertical offset is included.

Notes

  • The road is one element (a Direct Shape or a family instance). It is tagged with the alignment it follows, so running the command again for the same alignment replaces the earlier road, whichever way it was made before.
  • With Smooth surfaces, rounded parts of the section (arcs) are cut into short straight facets, because a smooth surface can only be straight across the section. Straight sides of the section are exact. Section spacing affects how closely the surfaces follow the alignment.
  • A family is a snapshot: it doesn't update when the alignment changes. Run the command again to replace it.
  • The section always stands upright, across the horizontal direction of the alignment, so a thickness stays a vertical thickness on a slope.
  • If a curve is tighter than the road is wide, the inside of the road would fold back on itself. Revit cannot build those spans, so they are left as gaps and the summary says how many; use a narrower section or a larger radius.
  • Not included yet: a section that changes along the road, banking (superelevation), junctions, and cutting the road itself into the topography (the sides can be graded to the ground, but the ground under the road is left as it is). To turn a road into a toposolid, use Sweep to Toposolid.
